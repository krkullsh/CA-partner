### 1\. THE PHILOSOPHY — WHY OPEN TIME, NOT A CALENDAR

Most study planners fail because they attach a chapter to a date instead of to a level of mastery. If you finish Chapter 5 in four days instead of the planned seven, a date-bound planner leaves you with three wasted days; if it takes you ten, you fall permanently behind and panic-skip later chapters. This strategy deliberately removes every date and week-marker. Every chapter is instead defined by a completion condition — a fixed number of MCQs solved correctly, a fixed number of full written answers produced, and a fixed number of oral recall cycles completed. You move to the next chapter only when the current one's targets are met, regardless of whether that took two days or nine.

This also solves the single biggest logical flaw in most planners: treating every chapter as equal. A chapter worth an average of 17 marks every attempt and a chapter worth 5 marks every attempt cannot receive the same number of written questions or oral revisions — doing so wastes your scarcest resource, which is not time but attention. Every number in this document (MCQs, one-liners, descriptive answers, mock attempts, oral recall cycles) is therefore derived mathematically from actual ICAI examination data across 7 attempts (May 2024 to May 2026), not from a generic template.

### THE ANALYTICAL FRAMEWORK — HOW EVERY TARGET NUMBER WAS DERIVED

Two independent data sources were combined to build this strategy: your 11-chapter Auditing & Ethics syllabus structure, and a 7-attempt examiner trend analysis (May 2024, Sep 2024, Jan 2025, May 2025, Sep 2025, Jan 2026, May 2026). Because ICAI's own trend sheet organises marks by individual Standard on Auditing (SA), while your syllabus organises the same standards into 11 combined chapters, the first step was to re-aggregate every SA's 7-attempt mark history into the 11-chapter structure you actually study from. This re-aggregation — not available in either source document individually — is what produces the true 'average marks per attempt' for each chapter, and this average is the primary driver of every practice target below.

The Priority Index — Three Factors, Not One A chapter's priority is not decided by average marks alone. Three factors are combined:

- Weightage (primary factor): Average marks scored by this chapter's standards across all 7 attempts. This decides the base tier.

- Frequency (consistency factor): How many of the 7 attempts actually tested this chapter. A topic tested in 7/7 attempts is a guaranteed scorer; one tested in 2/7 is a bonus-round topic.

- Momentum (trend factor): Whether the topic is rising, falling, or newly revived in the most recent 2-3 attempts. A topic like SA 530 — absent once, then tested twice running in both MCQ and descriptive form — is treated as a rising priority even though its 7-attempt average looks moderate.

These three factors combine into four practice tiers, and every chapter below is explicitly tagged with its tier and the reasoning behind that tag — so you always know why you are doing 50 MCQs for one chapter and 15 for another, instead of following an unexplained number.
